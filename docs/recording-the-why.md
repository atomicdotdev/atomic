# **Recording the Why**

*A foundation is defined by the metadata it can hold.*

---

## **The problem**

Git's own manual calls it a stupid content tracker. That's accurate. It records what bytes changed and understands none of it. For twenty years that was fine, because a person read every change and held the reason in their head. The diff was a reminder, and the understanding lived in the author.

Your agents work at machine speed, and they don't hold anything in their heads between turns. When the person left the loop, the understanding left with them. So now you have a history that records what changed and has no idea why, generated by something that can't tell you later.

You can ask git what a line says. You can't ask it why the line exists, who decided it should, or what an agent did to put it there.

We record the why. Every intent carries the reason behind it. Every change carries proof of who made it and the decision that motivated it. Every agent action carries a step-by-step ledger you can question after the fact and hand to a regulator without reconstructing anything. That's the whole job. This document is how we do it.

---

## **Where this comes from**

The shape we landed on isn't new. We rederived it from first principles, and then realized we'd been here before.

In the late 1990s, Peter Coad introduced color modeling as part of Feature-Driven Development. The central idea was deceptively simple: classify every object in a domain model into one of four archetypes, each color-coded. The one that carried the most weight was the pink archetype, the moment-interval. A moment-interval records that something happened, when it happened, and who was involved. It's the thing the business needs to remember.

Read our Decision node back. It's a moment-interval. A thing that happened, at a timestamp, with an author and a reason you're obligated to keep. We didn't borrow that from Coad. We built it by asking what a semantic change graph has to capture to make agent-speed development trustworthy, and arrived at the same structure. That's usually the sign the underlying shape is real.

FDD's deeper contribution was the feature template: `<action> <result> <object>`. That template is a tiny schema. It's a forcing function on how you're allowed to express a unit of work, and a malformed feature was supposed to get rejected at the modeling stage. Coad was reaching for structure-as-discipline thirty years ago. What he never had was a validator that enforced it. FDD's structure was advisory. A person read the feature, a person decided it was well-formed, and the structure lived in a document and a progress spreadsheet rather than in anything that could reject. That's the entire delta. We're making load-bearing the thing FDD could only ask someone to honor.

There's a second piece of Coad's lineage that closes the circle more tightly. Coad founded TogetherSoft, and Together's signature trick was round-trip engineering: keeping the model and the code as two synchronized projections of one truth. That is exactly the problem at the center of this document. Markdown in, typed node canonical, rendered view out, edits reconciling back. We spent months rediscovering round-trip engineering, except our canonical artifact is the change graph instead of a UML model, and the surface is markdown with directives instead of a class diagram.

The discipline FDD could only ask people to follow is the discipline agents structurally require. That's the through-line. The colored model was a typed semantic node two decades before the industry had the phrase, and agents need exactly that typing because they can't carry meaning between turns the way the person reading every diff used to.

## **The Knowledge Flywheel**

Intents, memories, and provenance aren't three features. They're a cycle.

A memory captures durable context: a constraint, a prior decision, a lesson. When someone (or an agent) starts a new piece of work, the relevant memories inform the intent. The intent records what we're going to do, why, and what we rejected. The intent motivates a change. The change generates provenance: who did it, what they used, what they produced, step by step. And the provenance, once reviewed, produces new memories. The constraint held. The assumption was wrong. The approach worked for this case but won't generalize. Those observations become memories that inform the next intent.

That's the flywheel. Memories inform decisions, decisions motivate changes, changes produce provenance, provenance generates memories. Each revolution makes the next one smarter because the graph carries what was learned, where it was learned, and why the learning was trusted. The system remembers for you instead of relying on whoever happened to be in the room.

The flywheel only works if every link is a real edge in the graph, traversable and queryable. If memories float as documents unconnected to the intents they informed, the cycle breaks and you're back to a pile of files someone has to reconstruct meaning from. And it only works if the nodes are honest. A faked reason breaks the cycle the same way a corrupted input breaks a feedback loop: everything downstream of it is built on something false, and nothing in the system can tell you where the contamination started unless the provenance chain is intact enough to trace back through.

The flywheel is also the reason memory and intent have to be separate node types with separate revision histories, rather than sections of one big document. Memories live longer than any single intent. A constraint like "no single ordering authority for multi-region writes" will inform dozens of intents over the life of the system. If that memory is embedded in the first intent that captured it, every subsequent intent either duplicates it or links to a section inside a document that's really about something else. Keeping them as independent nodes with their own ids, their own revision chains, and their own proofs means the flywheel's edges are clean and the cost of reuse is one reference, not a copy.

## **What we believe**

We arrived at these positions by building, not by theory. They're load-bearing, so they're worth stating plainly.

**The why is a typed node, not a text field.** A free-text comment can say anything, link to nothing, and contradict the change it describes. A typed node has an id you can resolve, edges you can traverse, and a shape a validator can reject. The reason becomes queryable because it's a node in the graph.

**There are three layers, and we keep them apart.** There's an authoring surface someone writes into, a canonical form that gets hashed and signed, and a render someone reads. Markdown is a surface. The typed node is canonical. HTML, CLI text, and editor panels are renders. Most confusion in this space comes from collapsing these. We don't collapse them.

**Presence is enforced. Content is left honest.** The gate requires that a reason exists, that authorship is proven, that the change links to its decision. The gate never grades the reason itself. The moment you put a shape on the prose, agents fill the shape and the reason becomes a box that's checked rather than a reason that's given. An empty why we can see beats a fake why we trust. We'd rather the field be visibly missing than convincingly faked.

**Every fact has one authoring site.** A fact written in two places can disagree, and the copy that stays as prose is invisible to a graph check. So status is written once, in the spine, and everywhere else it appears it's rendered from that one source. Contradiction stops being a thing we catch and becomes a thing that can't be represented.

**We reference, we don't embed.** A linked memory is a node with an id, and an intent points at it with a typed edge. We don't copy the memory's bytes into the intent. Copying freezes a snapshot that goes stale the moment the source revises. The link names a node and pins it to a revision. The reader sees the linked content inline because the render resolves the edge at read time. We store the edge. We inline at the render.

**The vocabulary is closed.** Node types, edge types, status values, and directive names come from a fixed registry. An unrecognized type is a gate error, not a new type the system quietly absorbs. Open vocabularies rot into something you can query syntactically and can't trust semantically. Closure is what makes this an ontology instead of a pile of typed JSON.

## **The three things we record**

### **Intent**

An intent is a decision and the reason behind it. What you want to happen, why, what you considered and rejected, and what proves you're the one who decided. It's the `entry_type: intent` we already ship. The intent is the moment-interval, in Coad's terms. The pink box. It records that someone decided, when they decided, what they decided, and why.

An intent carries collection data as typed sub-nodes: acceptance criteria, tasks, scope boundaries, constraints, and dependencies. These aren't prose sections, they're nodes in the graph, lifted from container directives in the body. Each one is individually addressable, individually attestable, and individually queryable. "Show me every acceptance criterion that's met but has no evidence link" is a graph query, not a grep.

### **Memory**

A memory is durable, reusable context. A constraint, a preference, an architectural decision worth carrying forward, a lesson learned from a previous change. Memories are append-only. You supersede a memory with a new revision, you don't mutate it, so the chain of what was believed when stays intact.

Memories are typed by kind: `constraint`, `preference`, `lesson`, `context`. The kind is part of the vocabulary and the gate rejects an unrecognized kind. Each memory carries `about` edges that link it to the modules, services, or domains it's relevant to. Those edges are what the flywheel resolves when an agent asks "what do I need to know before changing this module."

The directionality rule keeps the graph clean: a node references its inputs, never its future uses. An intent names the memories that informed it through an `informedBy` edge. A memory never lists the intents that will consume it. This is what lets memories stay stable while intents accrete around them.

### **Provenance**

Provenance is the record of who did what, and it's the layer that makes the other two trustworthy. Without provenance, an intent is a claim. With provenance, it's a claim you can verify by walking the chain.

We use the W3C PROV vocabulary because a regulator's tooling already speaks it. Every agent action is a `prov:Activity` (a turn), associated with a `prov:SoftwareAgent` that `actedOnBehalfOf` a `prov:Person`. The activity records what it `used` (the memories, the intent, the vault state) and what it `generated` (the change). Each turn's `turnParent` points at the prior turn, so the session is a hash-linked sequence, the journey rather than the endpoint.

The provenance graph for a change is a named subgraph, one addressable, signable unit you can hand to an auditor whole. It answers "what did the agent do, what did it rely on, and who was it acting for" without anyone reconstructing it after the fact.

## **Collection data on the graph**

Intents carry structured collections as first-class sub-nodes. These are the acceptance criteria, tasks, scope items, constraints, and dependencies that live inside an intent today as markdown sections with checkboxes. We lift them to typed nodes because the checkbox alone can't carry the weight they need to carry.

### **Acceptance criteria**

A criterion starts as a prose statement of what "done" means. When it's satisfied, it has to carry who verified it, when, and what evidence links to the change that proves it. A bare `[x]` records none of that. So the directive form carries the claim as an attribute, and the lift turns it into a node the gate can check.

```
:::acceptance-criterion{#WORD-5-ac-1 status=met verifiedBy=did:atomic:lee evidence=urn:atomic:change:01J8ZE7G2W}
On first load, the app presents a modal asking for the player's name.
:::
```

The prose inside the fences is the criterion's text, unconstrained. The attributes are the spine. The SHACL shape says: if `status=met`, then `verifiedBy` and `evidence` must be present and resolve to real nodes. A checked box without evidence fails the gate.

### **Tasks**

Tasks are the decomposed work items inside an intent. Each one names the files it touches, the criteria it addresses, and any dependency on other tasks. Today these are the `WORD-5/1`, `WORD-5/2` items in the TODOs section. As nodes, they carry typed edges to the files, the criteria, and each other.

```
:::task{#WORD-5-1 status=done criteria=WORD-5-ac-1}
Add name capture state, submit handling, modal markup, and top-of-page display.

::file-ref{path=src/App.tsx}
:::
```

The `criteria` edge links this task to the acceptance criterion it satisfies. The `::file-ref` is a leaf directive (no prose body) naming the file touched. `depends` edges between tasks are explicit and the gate can check for cycles.

### **Scope**

Scope records what's in and what's explicitly out. The "out" items are as important as the "in" items, because they're the decisions the agent is supposed to respect as boundaries. Today these are prose under a `## Scope` heading. As nodes, the gate can enforce that an out-of-scope declaration exists and that changes don't touch modules explicitly excluded.

```
:::scope-in
`src/App.tsx` React state and markup for the name modal and header display.
:::

:::scope-out
Persisting the name across browser reloads. Server-side user accounts or multiplayer identity.
:::
```

### **Constraints**

Constraints are rules the implementation must respect. They're authored as prose but typed as a collection so the graph knows how many there are and the render can present them as a checklist an agent can verify against.

```
:::constraint
Keep the implementation local to the existing React/Tailwind app.
:::

:::constraint
The modal should avoid interfering with global game keyboard handling by stopping
form keyboard events from bubbling to the window listener.
:::
```

### **Dependencies**

External dependencies (other intents, other systems, blocking decisions) are reference edges. They use the `:::ref` directive with a typed edge name so the graph can traverse the dependency chain and the gate can check that the target resolves.

```
:::ref{to=urn:atomic:intent:019efe80-1234 edge=blockedBy}
:::
```

### **The rule across all collections**

Every collection item is either a container directive (carries prose) or a leaf directive (carries only edges). The directive name is the type, the attributes are the spine, and the prose is the narrative. A type not in the registry is a gate error. This is the closed-vocabulary rule applied at the sub-node level.

## **The authoring surface**

Someone (or an agent) writes markdown. Frontmatter is the spine. The body is the narrative. Typed sub-nodes inside the body are container directives, not positional guesses inferred from heading structure.

### **Why markdown**

Agents emit markdown well under load. It's the one format where both people and machines have fluency, and fluency at the authoring surface is what decides whether the forcing function gets real input or gets gamed. A format the author fights is a format the author fakes.

### **Why frontmatter**

The spine lives in YAML frontmatter at the top of the file. It holds the facts the gate checks and the graph indexes: id, entry type, status, priority, authorship, the content hash, timestamps, and the edges to other nodes (motivated by, informed by, supersedes). Every spine field has exactly one authoring site, the frontmatter, and everywhere else it appears it's rendered from that source.

### **Why container directives**

Before directives, the body's structure was positional. A `- [x]` line under a `## Acceptance Criteria` header became a criterion because of where it sat. That positional lift is fragile. Rename the header, reorder the sections, nest something, and the parser silently mislifts or drops.

A container directive makes the type explicit and local at the point of authoring. The lift reads the type off the directive name instead of inferring it from position. That's the same move we keep making everywhere else: turn a convention the agent is supposed to honor into structure the system can read directly.

The three directive forms correspond to three node situations. Container directives (`:::name{...} ... :::`) wrap prose, a criterion with its description, a memory with its body. Leaf directives (`::name{...}`) carry only edges, a file reference, a dependency link. Inline directives (`:name[...]{...}`) name a node inside running prose, like referencing another decision mid-sentence in the why. The directive shape mirrors whether the node carries narrative or not.

Directive names are drawn from the closed registry. `:::acceptance-criterion`, `:::task`, `:::scope-in`, `:::scope-out`, `:::constraint`, `:::ref`, `::file-ref`. An unrecognized directive name is a gate error. The authoring surface is exactly as closed as the ontology behind it.

### **Why references instead of transclusion**

A `:::ref` directive points at another node by id and optionally pins it to a content-hash revision. It does not splice the target's bytes into the host file. Transclusion (copying the target's content into the host at parse time) freezes a snapshot. The memory revises, the host's embedded copy goes stale, and the content hash now attests a document whose included content no longer matches the source. That's the status-contradiction bug one layer up, and it's worse because the staleness is invisible.

The link names a node. The render inlines the content at read time. We store the edge. We show the content. They're not the same operation, and keeping them apart is the whole point.

## **The canonical form**

The authored markdown lifts to typed nodes in JSON-LD. JSON-LD is RDF serialized as JSON, so the nodes are triples the graph indexes, the linked-data toolchain understands, and a regulator's tooling can process. Nobody hand-writes the canonical form. It's a compile target, the same way nobody hand-writes the AST of their TypeScript.

### **Why JSON-LD and not the other options**

We evaluated markdown alone, XML, HTML, and HTML with custom elements.

Markdown has no schema, no canonicalization standard, and trailing-whitespace variation changes bytes without changing meaning. It's a poor thing to Merkle-hash. It's a good authoring surface and a bad canonical form.

HTML's parser is specified to never reject. Unclosed tags, misnested elements, and missing close tags all get silently fixed into a DOM. A browser will never refuse a malformed intent record; it renders something. That's the opposite of a forcing function. When we said "it's a file like a TypeScript file," the property we were actually reaching for was a compiler that rejects ill-typed input. HTML is the only option with no rejecting compiler. Its compiler is the browser, and the browser is engineered to never reject.

XML has the properties we need (draconian parsing, XSD schemas, C14N canonicalization, XML-DSig), but agents emit XML less reliably than JSON, and the verbosity invites box-checking.

JSON-LD gives us `@type` for node types, `@id` for stable identity, typed references as first-class edges, JSON Schema for validation, and JCS (RFC 8785\) for deterministic bytes to hash. Agents emit JSON well under load. And because JSON-LD is RDF, we get the PROV vocabulary for provenance and the Data Integrity proof standard for signing, both of which are native to the linked-data ecosystem.

### **The lift**

The lift is a typed extractor that reads the authored markdown and produces canonical nodes. Frontmatter keys map to spine properties. Directive names map to node types. Directive attributes map to typed properties and edges. Prose inside directive fences becomes the unconstrained text slot. Everything the lift doesn't have a rule for stays in the body as unlifted narrative, and that's correct, because the moment you try to schematize the why you're back to the Copilot Summary failure.

The lift has one overriding rule: a fact is lifted from exactly one site. If it appears in two places in the surface (like status in both the frontmatter and a body header), only the authoritative site is lifted. The other is ignored during the lift and regenerated during the projection back out. This is how we make contradiction unrepresentable rather than catchable.

The `turnParent` edge links to the prior turn, making the session a hash-linked sequence.

## **The gate**

A SHACL shape decides whether a node may enter the graph. This is the forcing function in the literal sense. `status: done` isn't a value an agent writes, it's a state the system grants when the shapes pass.

SHACL is written in Turtle, the same RDF the graph serializes to. The shapes live in the same data model as the nodes they check. The vocabulary you'll use most of the time is small: `sh:in` for closed value sets, `sh:minCount` and `sh:maxCount` for cardinality, `sh:class` to require that a reference resolves to a node of the right type, `sh:node` to say a referenced node must itself satisfy another shape, and `sh:sparql` for the conditional rules core SHACL can't express.

### **The intent shape**

```
@prefix sh:   <http://www.w3.org/ns/shacl#> .
@prefix atom: <https://atomic.dev/ns#> .
@prefix prov: <http://www.w3.org/ns/prov#> .
@prefix sec:  <https://w3id.org/security#> .

atom:IntentShape
    a sh:NodeShape ;
    sh:targetClass atom:Intent ;

    sh:property [
        sh:path atom:status ;
        sh:in ( "backlog" "todo" "in_progress" "done" ) ;
        sh:minCount 1 ; sh:maxCount 1 ;
        sh:message "status must be exactly one of the known states" ] ;

    sh:property [
        sh:path atom:attributedTo ;
        sh:minCount 1 ; sh:class prov:Agent ;
        sh:message "author must resolve to a known agent identity, not a string" ] ;

    sh:property [
        sh:path sec:proof ;
        sh:minCount 1 ;
        sh:message "intent must carry a Data Integrity proof" ] ;

    sh:property [
        sh:path atom:hasAcceptanceCriterion ;
        sh:node atom:AcceptanceCriterionShape ] ;

    sh:property [
        sh:path atom:hasTask ;
        sh:node atom:TaskShape ] .
```

### **The acceptance criterion shape**

```
atom:AcceptanceCriterionShape
    a sh:NodeShape ;

    sh:property [
        sh:path atom:acStatus ;
        sh:in ( "open" "met" ) ;
        sh:maxCount 1 ] ;

    sh:sparql [
        sh:message "a met acceptance criterion must carry verifiedBy and evidence" ;
        sh:select """
            PREFIX atom: <https://atomic.dev/ns#>
            SELECT $this WHERE {
                $this atom:acStatus "met" .
                FILTER NOT EXISTS {
                    $this atom:verifiedBy ?who .
                    $this atom:evidence   ?ev .
                }
            }""" ] .
```

### **The memory shape**

```
atom:MemoryShape
    a sh:NodeShape ;
    sh:targetClass atom:Memory ;

    sh:property [
        sh:path atom:memoryKind ;
        sh:in ( "constraint" "preference" "lesson" "context" ) ;
        sh:minCount 1 ; sh:maxCount 1 ;
        sh:message "memory kind must be one of the known types" ] ;

    sh:property [
        sh:path atom:status ;
        sh:in ( "active" "superseded" "retracted" ) ;
        sh:minCount 1 ; sh:maxCount 1 ] ;

    sh:property [
        sh:path atom:attributedTo ;
        sh:minCount 1 ; sh:class prov:Agent ;
        sh:message "author must resolve to a known agent identity" ] ;

    sh:property [
        sh:path sec:proof ;
        sh:minCount 1 ;
        sh:message "memory must carry a Data Integrity proof" ] .
```

### **The status lesson, because it's the whole thesis in miniature**

We found this bug in our own demo and it's worth walking through in detail, because every design choice in this document traces back to it.

An agent was given a task: implement a feature, then update the intent file to reflect completion. The intent file had status recorded in two places. The YAML frontmatter at the top carried `status: done`. And the body of the markdown, in a human-readable header line, carried `**Status:** backlog`. Below both of those, every acceptance criterion was checked and every TODO was marked complete. The agent had finished the work, updated the frontmatter to `done`, and left the body header untouched at `backlog`.

The file was internally contradictory, and nothing caught it. The frontmatter was valid YAML. The body was valid markdown. The content hash was computed over the whole file, so it faithfully attested a document that said two different things about the same fact. The contradiction shipped, cryptographically signed.

The first instinct is "add a rule that says there can be only one status." That's `sh:maxCount 1` in SHACL. It doesn't work. When the lift pulls the frontmatter status into the graph as one property and the body-header status into the graph as a different property, each property genuinely holds one value. The count is satisfied. The contradiction passes.

The second instinct is smarter: check agreement. SHACL's `sh:equals` says two properties must hold the same value. That catches the disagreement, because `done` is not equal to `backlog`, and the gate rejects the file.

But the third option is the one we chose, and it's the one that matters. We removed the second authoring site entirely. Status is authored once, in the frontmatter spine. The body-header line that used to say `**Status:** backlog` is no longer written by the agent or the person. It's rendered from the spine value when someone reads the file. The agent can't set it to a contradictory value because the agent doesn't write it at all. Contradiction stops being a thing we catch and becomes a thing that can't be represented.

Make the fact singular where you can. Check agreement where you can't. That's the principle that runs under every design choice in this document.

### **What the gate refuses to do**

The gate will never auto-fix a load-bearing fact. It may regenerate a projected field (a rendered body header) because nothing's invented there. It will never reconcile a contradiction in an attested fact by picking a winner. A gate that repairs the agreement it was meant to verify is manufacturing the thing we're trying to prove.

## **How you use it**

### **From the CLI**

**Scaffold a new intent from a template:**

```
atomic intent new WORD-5 --template feature
```

Produces a markdown file with the frontmatter spine pre-filled (id, view, author, timestamps) and the directive blocks stubbed for the template's expected collection types. The template is how the closed vocabulary reaches the author. You can only scaffold types that exist in the registry.

**Read an intent:**

```
atomic intent show WORD-5
```

Resolves the node and prints a rendered view as formatted text. Referenced memories are pulled in inline. No file is written, no browser is opened. The render is a pure function of the node, the way `git show` turns a stored object into a readable diff.

**Validate an intent:**

```
atomic intent validate WORD-5
```

Runs the SHACL gate against the lifted node and prints the report. On failure you get the failing node, the shape it broke, and the message. The same check runs automatically at the transition to `done`, so validate-by-hand is for authoring. The gate is for the system.

**Show the flywheel chain for a change:**

```
atomic provenance trace urn:atomic:change:01J8ZE7G2W
```

Walks the provenance graph: the change, the activity that generated it, the intent that motivated it, the memories that informed the intent, and the agent and person involved. This is the "prove the full chain without reconstructing it" claim from the pitch, made into a command.

**Scaffold a new memory:**

```
atomic memory new --kind constraint --about storage,replication
```

Produces a memory file with the kind, about edges, and authorship pre-filled. The body is open for the constraint text.

### **Templates**

A template is a directive scaffold tied to a node type and a use case. The `feature` template for an intent stubs acceptance criteria, tasks, scope, and constraint blocks. A `bugfix` template might stub a reproduction section and a root-cause memory reference. Templates are where authoring convenience and the closed vocabulary meet: the author gets a head start, and the head start can only contain types the ontology recognizes.

Templates don't constrain the body. They constrain the spine and the directive blocks. The prose inside each block is always unconstrained, because the moment you template the reason you're templating the fake.

### **The validation loop for agents**

The agent is the main consumer of a failed gate, so failures come back as a graph, not as prose the agent has to parse. The loop is:

1. Agent proposes a node (writes or updates the markdown).  
2. The lift extracts the canonical node.  
3. The gate runs SHACL shapes against the lifted node.  
4. On failure, the validation report (itself a graph: failing node, source shape, offending value, message) returns to the agent as structured input.  
5. The agent fixes the surface and resubmits.  
6. Only a passing node transitions.

No person sits in the inner loop unless a shape is written to demand one. The gate decides. The agent responds to the gate's structured output. The person authors the shapes that define what "right" means, and the system enforces them at machine speed.

## **What we are not building**

Saying no here is as load-bearing as anything above. Each of these is a road we looked down and refused.

**HTML as a source of truth.** HTML's parser is built to never reject. It recovers from malformed input and renders something, which is the opposite of a forcing function. We render to HTML for reading. We never store the canonical record as HTML.

**Hand-authored JSON-LD or XML on disk.** The canonical form is a compile target. People and agents author markdown with directives, because that's what they emit cleanly. JSON-LD is what the graph, the regulator, and the control plane consume. We don't ask anyone to hand-write the canonical node.

**Pre-rendered HTML files for every node.** We don't eagerly materialize renders. A system with 10,000 intents and 5,000 memories doesn't generate 15,000 HTML files. The render runs at the moment of a read, for the one node someone opened, and it's thrown away after. The CLI renders to text, the editor renders to a panel, the web view renders to HTML. One projection function, three output targets, zero files on disk.

**A triplestore as our storage tier.** The semantic-web stack wants us to land everything in a triplestore and query with SPARQL. That fights our patch-theory storage primitive head-on. The patch graph stays canonical. The triple view is a projection we compute and discard, the same relationship the regional redb materializations have to the S3 log.

**OWL reasoning and open-world inference.** OWL is where teams disappear into ontology engineering and never ship. Our gate is closed-world by nature: if the why is absent, we reject. SHACL validates what's present. We don't run a reasoner that invents facts we never asserted.

**Transclusion of linked content into the canonical form.** We reference by id and resolve at the render. We never splice another node's bytes into a host and then hash the host, because that freezes a snapshot that goes stale and makes our proof attest something that's no longer true.

**A schema on the reason itself.** The why is unconstrained prose, and it stays that way. Required structure on a reason produces a filled box that fakes the reason. That's the Copilot Summary problem: a reverse-engineered rationale that suppresses further inquiry by pretending the why was recorded when it was generated. An empty field is better than a convincing fake, because the empty field is at least visibly incomplete.

**Auto-fixing of load-bearing facts at the gate.** The gate may regenerate a projected field. It will never reconcile a contradiction in an attested fact by picking a winner. A gate that repairs the agreement it was meant to verify is manufacturing the thing we're trying to prove.

**An open directive or edge vocabulary.** An agent being able to invent `:::whatever` and have it lift to a node is how the graph rots. Unknown names are gate errors. The directive surface is exactly as closed as the ontology behind it.

**`maxCount` as a contradiction check.** A cardinality constraint ("there can be only one status") rejects redundancy, not disagreement. Two properties can each hold one value and still contradict each other. The rule passes a file that says `done` in one place and `backlog` in another. Where two sources must match, we check agreement. Where we can, we make the fact singular so there's nothing to disagree with and no rule is needed.

**SPARQL as a general-purpose constraint language.** The SPARQL escape hatch in SHACL is powerful and it's the part to ration. Every rule we can express with `sh:in`, `sh:class`, or `sh:equals` stays declarative, portable, and cheap to reason about. We reach for `sh:sparql` only for genuinely conditional rules and resist letting the gate grow into a query engine.

## **The one rule, if you remember nothing else**

Make the fact singular where you can, so contradiction can't be expressed. Check agreement where you can't. Reference, don't embed. Enforce that the reason exists, and leave what it says alone.

The discipline FDD could only ask someone to follow is the discipline agents structurally require. We're making load-bearing the thing that used to be advisory. That's the pink box, thirty years later, with a validator that finally rejects.
